Step 2

Insights analysis

Events become insights when they are compared: this moment against the class's usual, the same moment across the week, and weeks against weeks.

Three kinds of insight

Now

This event against this class's usual for the same moment: "Back from recess, 10 of 21 unsettled; usually 6."

Pattern

The same moment across the week: Recess is unsettled four days out of five. This is the insight that opens Activity execution.

Trend

Weeks against weeks, with and without an activity: is the recess pattern easing since wall pushes started? Step 4 measures it.

Activity execution

Start from the insight result, see the shift the room needs, then the activities that can make that shift: what each is, how it is run, and the science behind why it should help.

2. The map: which activity families fit which result

A full dot is a first choice for that result; a light dot is a supporting option. Tap a result or a family to go deeper.

What the evidence says, in one line

Movement breaks and slow breathing have the best evidence; heavy work, Brain Gym style moves and weighted vests the weakest. Almost nothing has been tested on a class that is already Red, within minutes: RegulateED's own re-checks could be the first.

Teacher aids, for every activity
  • A short script to read aloud, with a story for K to 2
  • A visual and a 2-minute timer on the teacher's screen
  • Grade adaptations and the cautions
  • A re-check prompt when it ends

3. How the options are offered

The order of the options
  • After the check-in the teacher sees 2 or 3 activities to choose from, then a re-check at the end.
  • The best-supported activity comes first, with 2 or 3 more behind it to "mix it up", each showing its evidence grade.
  • Each choice comes from a different activity family, and each is the strongest in its family.
  • The top two rotate, so the class does not tire of one.
  • The order follows what works for this class, from its own re-checks, and updates every Monday morning. One that works stays on top.
  • A teacher can pin an activity. A pinned one that is not working gets a note beside it.

Foundations

The event record: what every check-in, activity and re-check stores.

An event has to know whether an activity followed it and what it aimed for, or no trend can measure the activity. That is the one change to the data from today's design.

Event (check-in)

  • Moment, date and time
  • One of three standard moments: start of day, after recess, after lunch
  • Teacher, class, grade
  • Method (1 to 5)
  • Children present
  • Counts: Green, Yellow, Red, or the class color
  • Purpose: check-in

Activity run

  • Responds to: the event
  • Activity and family
  • Led by: teacher, or the app per child (G4, G5)
  • Started, length

Expectation

  • The aim: fewer Reds and Yellows, no fixed target
  • Re-check right after the activity
  • Real numbers set by the pilot

Event (re-check)

  • Same fields as a check-in
  • Purpose: re-check
  • Linked to the activity run
  • K to G3: the teacher's read (class color, Voices, Bodies, Ready to start)

Outcome

  • Actual shift: re-check minus check-in
  • Met or not met
  • Feeds effectiveness by class, moment, activity

Fields in coral are new: they exist so trends can measure activities.

The baseline

Check-ins with no activity run are kept just the same. They are the "usual" an insight compares against, and the weeks without an activity a trend compares with.

Still nothing about a child

Every record is about the class and the moment. G4 and G5 answers arrive unnamed; whether they carry an anonymised token is still to decide.

Foundations

How we grade the evidence for each activity.

Strong

Several good trials or a meta-analysis in children, consistent on outcomes that matter in class: behavior, attention, measured stress.

Moderate

Good evidence, but mostly in adults, in small trials, or mixed in children. Reasonable to use; worth measuring.

Emerging

Promising early studies, a plausible mechanism or a strong practice tradition, not yet tested well in classrooms.

Weak or contested

Reviews find little or no effect, or its claims are disputed. Use with care, never as a promise, and say so.

Why it matters

A health product for children must not claim more than the science supports. The full review with every source is in the repo: 2026-11-18 RegulateED activity evidence review.

Method 1

Teacher-led group question

The teacher asks the whole class and reads the room, then records one color for the class.

  1. The teacher opens the event: Recess, today, their class.
  2. Asks the class, "How are we feeling?"
  3. Children answer out loud, hold up a color card or point.
  4. The teacher picks one color for the class. Saved.
Why K and G1

No reading and no device in a child's hand. It is what a K or G1 teacher already does, and it takes under a minute.

What is recorded

One color for the class, saved to the event. Nothing about any child.

The teacher's read, before and after each reset

This is the one teacher step. Right before and right after each reset the teacher records the class color plus three taps: Voices, Bodies, Ready to start. In K to G3 it is the re-check, not a second child tap; K and G1 keep the child door check-in as well.

Method 2

Event-based entry survey

The teacher's iPad stands at the classroom door. As the children come in, each one taps the face that matches how they feel.

  1. The teacher opens the event and sets their iPad on a stand at the door.
  2. As the line comes in: "How are you feeling?"
  3. Each child taps one face as they pass. A soft chime says "got it".
  4. The teacher closes the event and sees the count of each color.
Why K to G3

Faces, not words, so no reading is needed, and every child counts the same. It stops after G3: older children are seen tapping by the line, and answer more honestly in private.

What is recorded

A count of each color and the total taps against the children present. Never a name, and never the time or order of a tap, so a tap cannot be matched to the child who walked in.

Private, anonymous, and the start of the day
  • The door tap stays private and anonymous, and each tap is counted separately.
  • There is no parent view.
  • At start of day the door tap is the "before"; after each reset the teacher's read is the re-check.

Method 3

QR code survey: anonymous, by event

The event's QR code goes up on the board. Children scan it on a classroom iPad or Chromebook and answer a short web form. No name, no login, no app.

  1. The teacher opens the event; RegulateED shows its QR code.
  2. Children scan it, or type the short link.
  3. Pick a color, then "Where do you feel it?"
  4. Send. The answer joins the event.
Why G2 to G4

Children now read simple words and can use a shared device, so each answers alone and adds one more thing: where they feel it. It stops after G4, when the app does everything the form does plus the reset.

What is recorded

One unnamed answer per child, pooled into the event: the color and the body spot. The QR code carries the event, never the child.

Joining: a shared door and a private ticket

For the QR form in G2 to G4, and for individual iPads in K to G3.

  • The QR code on the board is the shared door. It refreshes about every 45 seconds, and the previous code still works.
  • Each scan gives that iPad a one-time ticket for this event: the private ticket.
  • The teacher sees how many have joined against how many are in class, and taps Close.
  • Odd results are flagged, never deleted.
  • No device ID and no location.

We describe it as class-bounded and spam-resistant, never as verified.

From results to action

Every result becomes an insight, and every insight offers a way to shift the class.

The check-in tells the teacher where the class is. Insights turn that into what to do next: activities designed to move a mostly Red room toward Yellow and Green, offered as options the teacher chooses and applies to the class.

The insight

Read from the split, never the average alone: how much of the room is Red, Yellow or Green, at which moment, compared with this class's usual. For example, "Back from recess, almost half the class is unsettled: more than usual for them."

The options

Two or three activities designed for that result, each about 2 minutes and done at the desk or as a class, each from a different activity family and the strongest in its family. A mostly Red room starts with slow breathing together, with lower lights and a lower teacher voice; a mostly Yellow room gets soothing touch or breathing. The teacher chooses; RegulateED never runs one on its own. How the options are ordered →

A quick re-check after the activity is a second event linked to the first, so the shift is measured: Red to Yellow, Yellow to Green. In K to G3 the re-check is the teacher's read of the room. What insights learn over time

When

The moments a class usually struggles, such as after recess or before lunch, so a reset can be planned before the room tips into Red.

What works

Which activities shift this class, at which moment, ranked by the re-checks. The best one is offered first.

Progress

Whether the class needs fewer resets over the weeks: the trend a teacher, a school OT or a principal wants to see.

To develop

The RegulateED activity library: each activity designed by an OT for a result, an age band and a place (at the desk, standing, whole class). The examples above come from the framework's four groups: heavy work, movement across the body, touch resets and rhythmic breathing.

Agreed in Sprint 1

Green, Yellow and Red cannot show a sluggish, low-energy class, the one that needs waking up, not calming down. The framework used a fourth state, Blue, for that. The check-in stays Green, Yellow and Red, with no Blue button: the teacher spots a low-energy room.

7Privacy, plainly

On the slide

On the slide

Behind it

11The OT science behind it

On the slide

Four ideas, each with its honest strength. Interoception, noticing body signals (Mahler et al 2022 and 2024, Bishop et al 2023, Cangi and Bumin 2026: emerging). Sensory-based strategies and body awareness for self-regulation, with the Alert Program as the closest relative of a color check-in (Pfeiffer et al 2018: moderate). A tiered school model where whole-class supports are Tier 1 (Whiting et al 2025: emerging). And the frameworks in OT language: polyvagal theory, sensory integration, the Zones of Regulation and Person-Environment-Occupation, kept as Jordan asked.

What it must show

The science is sound, and it is stated honestly.

Behind it

The strong Ayres Sensory Integration evidence (Piller et al 2025, Acuña et al 2025, Schaaf et al 2018: moderate) tests one-to-one clinic therapy, not a 2-minute desk break, so it is OT background only. Two sources to recheck before the deck: Laborde 2022 and Obradovic 2021.

Part 6 · The pilot

15How a pilot runs in a school

On the slide

3 to 6 classrooms, a multiple baseline: check-ins alone for at least 5 school days, then the resets added, each class starting a week apart in a random order. Measures: the class colors before and after each reset, the teacher's read, one outcome a teacher or observer records (on-task time, minutes lost, or office referrals), how many planned resets were delivered, and what teachers say. Success criteria written before it starts, for example 80% of planned resets delivered and 75% of check-ins completed. It tests whether the program is feasible, not how big its effect is. Before it starts: OTs, teachers and children rate the activity material and it is revised, the privacy papers are done, the school and district approve, and the music rights are cleared.

What it must show

A school can say yes to a small, safe, well-designed trial.

Behind it

The pilot design research from Sprint 1 (strong: consensus methods standards, the What Works Clearinghouse handbook and the CONSORT pilot extension). Roan et al 2022: a cheap, respectable way to vet material with experts and end users before a pilot.
