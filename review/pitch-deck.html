The problem

Regulation breaks down at the desk, and it lands on the teacher.

Top 3

Student behavior is one of teachers' top three sources of stress, ahead of low pay, five years running.

RAND State of the American Teacher, 2026, as reported by EdWeek

When it happens: wired after recess, sluggish after lunch, overloaded by noise and crowding. The hardest moments for young children are social and outdoors (Booth et al 2019, abstract only).

Who is there: one teacher with a whole class. The OT who knows what helps is rarely in the room when it happens.

National figures for now. A short RegulateED teacher survey will add local numbers once it is run.

What RegulateED is

One short loop, at three moments of the school day.

1. Check in

The children answer one question: "How is your body feeling right now?" Green, Yellow or Red, anonymously.

→

2. Reset

RegulateED suggests the activity that best suits the class's result. The teacher picks, and Reggie leads it.

→

3. Check in again

As lessons continue, the teacher reads the vibe of the room in a few seconds and records how the class is now.

The three moments

Start of day, after recess and after lunch, the times classes most often come in unsettled.

Trends, for reference

The loop is the daily work. Trends are there for anyone who wants them: to see how the class changes, and for analysis at the end of the pilot.

Meet Reggie

The calm buddy who leads every reset on the classroom screen. He shows each move, does it with the class, and always finishes calm. In the younger grades he talks, in a young boy's voice.

What it includes

Something for the class, the teacher and the school OT.

The class

A check-in that takes seconds, and 2-minute resets on the classroom screen, led by Reggie.

The teacher

The class's split after each check-in (how many children chose each color), a top suggested activity with others behind it, the read of the room afterward, and the trends when wanted.

The school OT

Helps put RegulateED into the school, trains the teachers, and stays involved to make sure it runs smoothly.

How each grade checks in

Every age answers in the way it can.

Before the reset: the children's own answer

  • K and Grade 1: the teacher asks the class, or each child taps at the door on the iPad
  • Grades 2 and 3: the door tap, or an anonymous QR code form
  • Grade 4: the QR form, or the RegulateED app
  • Grade 5: the app, on a school iPad or in a browser

Every answer is tied to an event: the moment, the date, the teacher and the class.

After the reset: the teacher's read of the room

Lining the class up to tap again takes too long, so as lessons continue the teacher reads the vibe of the room in a few seconds and records the split, for example "most Green, a few Yellow".

The first check-in is the children's own answer and the second is the teacher's read. They are different kinds of measure, and the pilot analysis treats them that way.

Behind it: a very brief, anonymous, class-level check-in fits school entry better than a clinical screener (Barrett-Young et al 2024) moderate

What we do with the data

Each check-in becomes a choice the teacher can make in seconds.

This check-in

The teacher sees only this check-in's split, and the activities that suit it, the best supported first and the others behind it.

Trends that build

The trends update after every check-in, so the picture of the class grows as the weeks go on.

"Not steady yet"

Until the class has 8 check-ins at a moment, the trend says plainly that its average is not steady yet.

Why 8: one reset can go either way. Teacher ratings are steadier as an average over about eight resets (Wickerd and Hulac 2017), so RegulateED reads trends, not a single day.

The interventions

Three resets to start, each two minutes, each with its evidence.

Slow breathing

A long out-breath, following Reggie's belly as it grows and shrinks. The first choice for a mostly Red room, with lower lights and a lower teacher voice.

Movement break to a song

Reggie dances one easy move at a time and the class copies, winding down in the last 30 seconds before the check-in.

Heavy work

Muscle work such as pushing the hands together or chair push-ups, for a wired, restless body.

What the research says, honestly: short classroom breaks are feasible for teachers, and early results are promising for related outcomes, but no study has yet shown that a 2-minute reset improves regulation. The pilot is the test. emerging

In trials with grades 3 and 4, teacher-led 3 to 5 minute breathing breaks were followed by better arithmetic (Voltmer et al 2023) and steadier peer relationships (von Salisch and Voltmer 2023), and short active breaks were feasible and liked (Watson et al 2019). None measured regulation.

A design note: a mentally demanding movement break may hurt flexible thinking right afterward (Egger et al 2018, abstract only) moderate, so the resets stay simple.

The OT science behind it

Consistent with OT evidence, and honest about its strength.

Leading idea: sensory-based strategies and body awareness moderate

An AOTA systematic review rated self-regulation programs like the Alert Program as moderate evidence. The Alert Program's "engine levels" are the closest relative of RegulateED's Green, Yellow and Red check-in (Pfeiffer et al 2018). It was tested with children who have sensory processing challenges, not whole classes, so RegulateED builds on it rather than borrowing its results.

Interoception

Noticing body signals, the skill behind "How is your body feeling?" (Mahler et al 2022 and 2024, Bishop et al 2023, Cangi and Bumin 2026)

Privacy, ready for schools

Anonymous class results only. Never a record of one child.

What RegulateED keeps

  • Anonymous, class-level counts tied to an event
  • No names, no parent view, no device ID
  • No ads, and no selling or sharing of data
  • Deleted or returned within 90 days of the end
  • A named contact for every school

Before the St. Paul pilot

Minnesota's student data law (Minn. Stat. 13.32) covers a school's data even in a free trial. The pilot needs:

  • District and school approval, starting with the district's data privacy contact
  • A written agreement, a plain privacy page and a deletion process
  • Wording for the district's notice to parents

A research map, not legal advice. Keeping results anonymous and class-level keeps RegulateED clear of the heavier FERPA and COPPA duties that apply once a check-in is tied to one child.

Outcomes

What the pilot is built to test. No results exist yet.

  1. Self-regulation. Do children's own check-ins shift toward Green over the semester? What the program targets directly.
  2. Class climate. Does the teacher's read of the room improve after resets, and across weeks?
  3. Learning. An open question, not a promise: nothing yet ties a short break to better learning.

The pilot

One school in St. Paul, Minnesota: grades 1 to 5, 14 weeks.

Two classes in every grade

  • In each grade, 1 to 5, one class uses RegulateED and one is the comparison class: 10 classes in all
  • A coin flip decides which class is which, not the teachers
  • Both classes do identical check-ins, every school day: morning, after recess and after lunch
  • The comparison class gets no resets, and gets RegulateED when the pilot ends
  • A free trial, run with a school-based OT

14 weeks: 2 weeks of baseline, then resets start in two steps

Baseline
Half the RegulateED classes: resets from week 3
Baseline
The other half: resets from week 4
Comparison classes: check-ins only, all 14 weeks
  • Weeks 1 and 2: check-ins only for every class, about 30 per class
  • Weeks 3 and 4: the RegulateED classes start, half each week, picked at random
  • Weeks 4 to 14 (11 weeks) are compared for every class, against the same baseline

About 30 check-ins, not an exact number: holidays and field trips shorten some weeks. Grades 4 and 5 need school devices for the app.

The pilot

What it measures, and what it can and cannot show.

What it measures

  • The children's check-ins, in both classes of every grade
  • The teacher's read after each reset
  • One outcome a teacher records, such as minutes lost or on-task time
  • How many planned resets were run, and what teachers say

How it is compared

Grade by grade, the change from baseline in the RegulateED class against the change in the comparison class. Five grades give five matched pairs, and a result that points the same way in most of them is worth noting.

Success criteria are written down before it starts, for example 80% of planned resets run and 75% of check-ins completed.

Its honest limits

Each class has its own teacher, which is part of any difference, and five pairs is small. So this is a pilot comparison that shows whether a larger study is worth running, not proof that RegulateED works.
